\chapter{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This chapter discusses what the results mean for the two research questions and where the limits of our setup lie.


\section{Summary of Results}\label{sec:summary}

Our first research question asked how a character can stay on story when the user interrupts. Our answer is to separate deciding from performing. A Director that tracks the current beat and steers every turn back to it made the story more coherent in the eyes of our participants (3.93 vs.\ 1.71, about two points on a five-point scale) and harder to derail (4.42 vs.\ 1.58, both $p \le 0.002$, $n$=14 and 12 pairs). The character's voice did not suffer. The LLM judge saw the same advantage, and it held for a second character as well.

Our second question asked how a character can tell a complete story within a fixed time. Here, the key is to keep track of time outside the model. The Temporal Monitor tells the Director when to compress or skip less important beats. In our one 5-minute study story, the Time-Constrained Director reached a well-paced ending in 10 of 14 rounds (71\%), while 12 of 14 unconstrained rounds (86\%) were cut short. This is evidence from a single story and a single budget, not a general guarantee: in the LLM-judge time-budget ablation, quality fell sharply for budgets below about five minutes, so completion cannot be relied on at short budgets. Participants also found its endings more satisfying (\pval{0.016}), and we found no measurable cost on the other measures.

Beyond the two research questions, the user study shows three things. First, the Director's clearest effects are on coherence and on resisting derailment. Both differences are large and significant ($p \le 0.002$), and 11 of the 12 participants who tried to derail rated the Director higher on staying on track. Second, the two systems fail in different ways. When the user goes off topic, the baseline lets the story stall and often never returns to it. The Director keeps the story going, but some participants found the way it steers back rigid. Third, staying in character was never the problem. All three conditions scored between 4.00 and 4.29 on it. What differs is whether the story moves forward and how interruptions are handled.

\section{Narrative Progression vs.\ Natural Conversation}\label{sec:tradeoff}

The Director gets its control by always steering back to the current beat, and this is also its main limitation. Every turn the user spends on their own topic is a turn in which the story does not move forward, so the system has to decide how much of the conversation it gives up for the plot.

Our results show both sides of this trade-off. The baseline follows whatever the user says, but the story often derails (Section~\ref{sec:user-study-results}). The Director keeps the story going, yet some participants found its return to the script rigid. One of them wrote that ``at some point he should break this cycle''. Neither system gets both right.

The same tension appears in two further places. First, the standard Director never lets the user stop or skip the story, because it treats such requests as derailment. The lenient Director (Section~\ref{sec:extensions}) honours them after asking for confirmation, but we did not evaluate it. Second, the time limit adds its own cost. To finish on time, the Time-Constrained Director compresses or skips less important beats, and in the Cinderella study the last two beats never received a dedicated turn in any of the 14 transcripts.

Naturalness is the part we know least about. Only Story 2 asked whether the story felt like a genuine conversation (Q9), and both conditions scored low (2.93 and 2.64, \pval{0.27}, $n$=14 pairs). Story 1 had no such question, so we cannot directly compare how natural the Director feels against the baseline. How strictly the Director should hold to the story therefore depends on the application, and we leave this choice open.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Our results have several limits. We tested only two characters, Olaf and Rocket Raccoon, and mostly on Disney stories. We therefore cannot say how well the results carry over to other characters and genres. All variants use the same language model, so we cannot say whether larger models would be less prone to drift. The automated evaluation relies on a single judge model for most comparisons. Its scripted user inputs cannot react to what the character says, as a real user would, so they cannot test how natural a conversation feels. The time constrained user study used a single story and a single five minute budget. The beat generator and the lenient Director were built but not tested thoroughly. Finally, the user study is small. Only 14 of the 28 participants completed it, and we could not rule out that the dropout biased the results. Each participant also spent limited time with each condition.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}

This thesis set out to make LLM-based characters tell a story that stays on track and is more likely to finish within a time limit. Our system separates deciding from performing. A Director decides on every turn how to handle the user's input and where the story should go next, and the character carries out that decision in its own voice. In our tests, this split was an effective way to keep control of the narrative when users interrupt, and it costs only one extra model call per turn. For the time limit, we keep track of time outside the language model. A simple Temporal Monitor compares the story's progress with the time that has passed and tells the Director when to speed up or slow down.

Both evaluations support this design. In the user study, participants rated the Director's stories about 2.2 points higher on coherence and about 2.8 points higher on derailment resistance on a five point scale. In the LLM-as-Judge evaluation, where every pair of stories is judged in both orders to rule out position bias, the Director scored higher than the baseline on Narrative Control and Interruption Handling, with an Interruption Handling lead of 1.9 to 4.1 points depending on the derailment level.

\section{Future Work}

Several directions remain open. The first is to bring the system closer to a real interactive character. Integrating it with animation pipelines would let the character speak and move as part of a complete experience. For this to work in real time, the Director also needs to become faster, since its extra model call adds delay to every turn.

A second direction is the lenient Director. Some participants found the standard Director rigid because it steers back to the story no matter what they ask for. The lenient Director already honours requests to stop or skip after confirmation, and it could be developed further so that users feel more acknowledged, for example by taking up their ideas in the story instead of only redirecting them.

A third direction is memory. With it, a character could switch between stories, or a user between characters, without losing what happened before.

Finally, the evaluation can be extended. We would like to test the approach on other kinds of stories beyond Disney retellings, and to give the beat generator and the lenient Director a proper evaluation of their own.
