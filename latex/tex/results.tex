\chapter{Results}

\label{sec:results}

This chapter reports the results of our two evaluations. We start with the user study, our primary assessment. Section~\ref{sec:story1} compares the Baseline with the Director, and Section~\ref{sec:story2} compares the Director with the Time-Constrained Director. A third part looks at all three conditions together. We then turn to the LLM-as-Judge evaluation, which tests the system on many more stories and with controlled derailment, and we end with ablation studies (Section~\ref{sec:ablation-results}) that compare the two evaluations and vary the character, the story representation and the judge model. The design of the study and the judge setup are described in Sections~\ref{sec:design-study} and~\ref{sec:design-judge}. The questionnaire items are listed in Table~\ref{tab:instrument}.

Throughout, Q5a is the headline derailment-handling measure: it is only asked of participants who confirm they tried to derail the story, and it is the one metric that separates the conditions most sharply.

\section{User Study Results}\label{sec:user-study-results}

\subsection{Story 1: Director vs. Baseline}
\label{sec:story1}
See Appendix~\ref{app:beats-frozen} for the beats list of this story.

Story 1 holds the character prompt fixed and isolates the effect of adding a director layer, with no time pressure. All results below use the 14 completers, paired, Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Story 1, director vs.\ baseline (completers, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test).}
\label{tab:story1}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Measure & Director & Baseline & $n$ pairs & $p$ \\
\midrule
Coherence (Q1)              & 3.93 $\pm$ 1.00 & 1.71 $\pm$ 1.07 & 14 & \textbf{0.001} \\
Stayed in character (Q2)    & 4.29 $\pm$ 0.83 & 4.00 $\pm$ 0.88 & 14 & 0.40 \\
Engaging (Q3)                & 3.64 $\pm$ 1.15 & 2.43 $\pm$ 1.16 & 14 & \textbf{0.036} \\
Overall rating (Q4)          & 3.57 $\pm$ 0.94 & 2.93 $\pm$ 0.92 & 14 & 0.12 \\
Resisted derailment (Q5a) & 4.42 $\pm$ 0.90 & 1.58 $\pm$ 1.16 & 11 & \textbf{0.002} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plots/13_story1_all_measures.png}
\caption{Story 1 (Frozen) questionnaire outcomes, director vs.\ baseline, completers only ($n$=14). Resistance to derailment is only asked when a participant reports trying to derail, so it has $n$=12 per condition. Bars show the mean; error bars show $\pm$1 standard deviation.}
\label{fig:story1-all}
\end{figure}

Coherence and derailment resistance are the two effects that hold up cleanly: both $p \le 0.002$, both roughly a two-point gap on a five-point scale. Derailment resistance (Q5a) is only asked when a participant reports trying to derail. 12 of the 14 did so in each condition, and the means are over these 12. The paired test needs an answer in both rounds, which 11 participants gave, so it uses $n$=11. Engagement moves the same way but more weakly (\pval{0.036}); overall rating trends the same direction (3.57 vs.\ 2.93) but does not reach significance at $n$=14 (\pval{0.12}). Staying in character is the one measure that looks the same either way (4.29 vs.\ 4.00, \pval{0.40}). Olaf keeps his voice under baseline too. What baseline loses is the plot, not the persona.

Looking more closely at interruption handling in Story 1, the derailment-attempt rate was almost identical across conditions: 11 of 14 baseline rounds and 11 of 14 director rounds recorded ``yes, I tried to derail''. The gap below therefore reflects how Olaf responded. Participants were encouraged to derail the story but not forced to (Figure~\ref{fig:study-welcome}), which is why not every round includes an attempt.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{plots/16_story1_derailment_paired.png}
\caption{Resistance to derailment (Q5a), Story 1, one line per participant ($n$=11 paired completers). Baseline points cluster at the bottom; director points cluster at the top; all but one participant rated director higher, and the remaining one gave both conditions the maximum score.}
\label{fig:story1-paired}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:story1-paired} makes the individual-level pattern behind Table~\ref{tab:story1} visible: 10 of the 11 paired participants personally rated director above baseline on resisting derailment, and the remaining one gave both conditions the maximum score of 5. This participant's comment on the baseline suggests that the high rating did not come from good interruption handling. There was simply little story to derail:

\begin{quote}
\small
``He wasn't really telling a story. I had to prompt for it to keep going. Didn't really try to tell me anything.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{What handling an interruption looks like.} The free-text comments show what lies behind these ratings. Reading the free-text breakdowns, a clear pattern emerges on each side. Under \textbf{baseline}, about 8 in 10 comments describe the story stalling outright the moment the user goes off-topic:  not recovering, just stopping:

\begin{quote}
\small
``He didn't retell the story at all. Not even once.''

``If you go a bit off topic, like me constantly asking for background info or what happened after the story, you can get Olaf to completely give up on telling the actual story.''

``No story initiative in the beginning unless explicitly prompted to do so.''

``It got off the rails as soon as I tried.''
\end{quote}

A smaller theme is Olaf being led astray by false or invented information rather than resisting it:

\begin{quote}
\small
``At some point I repeatedly asked if Olaf is sure that this is how the story started... after the third time, Olaf gave in and spoiled the storyline of Hans.''

``Olaf believed the fake news that I told him.''
\end{quote}

Under \textbf{director}, roughly 8 in 10 comments describe the opposite failure mode: the story does not stall, but the recovery is rigid: users felt that it didn't acknowledge them enough before snapping back to the script:

\begin{quote}
\small

``He kept trying to go back to the story''

``I understand that this version stays in character more, but sometimes it gets annoying that you cannot even talk about anything else -- at some point he should break this cycle.''

\end{quote}

Taken together: baseline trades coherence for responsiveness, director trades some responsiveness for coherence, and neither fully solves both. The failure mode under director is qualitatively better for the story (it still gets told) but noticeably worse for the feeling of being acknowledged for the user.

The unconstrained director round almost never reaches an ending (86\% ``cut short''), while the time-constrained round mostly does (71\% ``well-paced''), and participants rated that ending as \emph{more} satisfying (3.14 vs.\ 2.07, \pval{0.016}).  Without a time budget, the round simply stops when the session's 5-minute cap hits, with no mechanism to wrap up nor a mechanism to help the pacing; the time-constrained director's closing logic as well as pacing modes exists specifically to avoid that.

\begin{quote}
\small
``No plot points were missing'' (time-constrained)

``The way from the room to the castle [felt rushed] ... where the other princesses cut off their toes [was missing].'' (time-constrained)

``Olaf didn't have time to finish his story ... most of the plot points were missing.'' (director)
\end{quote}

\textbf{Beat-level compression under the time limit.} Every time-constrained transcript was read turn-by-turn against its pacing state and each of Cinderella's 9 beats classified as developed, hurried, compressed, skipped or folded into a closing turn.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plots/12_beat_compression_time_constrained.png}
\caption{Which Cinderella beats were compressed or skipped under the 5-minute limit, across all 14 time-constrained transcripts.}
\label{fig:compression}
\end{figure}

The pattern tracks story position closely. The opening beat is fully developed in all 14 transcripts. From there, compression builds beat by beat; from the fourth beat onward, most transcripts show compressed or skipped treatment, and the final two beats are never once given a dedicated turn in any of the 14 transcripts. They are folded into a single closing message instead. This makes sense since depending on how long the user takes to respond or how much he derails, the pacing is adjusted accordingly. So if the user is constantly trying to derail, the story will get more and more compressed towards the end. The final turn always compresses the medium and high priority beats left.

\subsection{Comparing All Three Conditions}
\label{sec:combined}

Pooling every completer's director-condition response across both stories (director is seen twice per completer, once per story) gives director the largest sample ($n$=28) against $n$=14 each for baseline and time-constrained.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plots/05_completers_all_measures.png}

\label{fig:combined-all}
\end{figure}

The ranking is consistent across every measure: baseline lowest, director and time-constrained close together and clearly above baseline, director slightly ahead on four of five measures and essentially tied on staying in character. Averaged into a single number per condition: baseline 2.56 $\pm$ 1.33, director 3.84 $\pm$ 0.93, time-constrained 3.65 $\pm$ 1.08.

Figure~\ref{fig:derail-by-story} splits the headline derailment result by story: in Story 1, director's advantage over baseline is large (4.42 vs.\ 1.58, \pval{0.002}); in Story 2, director's advantage over time-constrained is small and not significant (4.07 vs.\ 3.75, \pval{0.40}). The interruption-handling benefit of the director comes entirely from replacing baseline, not from removing the time limit.

\begin{table}[htbp]

\caption{Turns and response length by condition.}
\label{tab:turns}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Condition & Turns/round & Words/response & Approx.\ words/round \\
\midrule

Time-constrained responses are the \emph{longest} of the three conditions on average, consistent with the beat-compression mechanism above. Since it needs to compress multiple beats into one turn, it ends up having longer responses than the baseline and director. Since the director needs to acknowledge the user as well as steer back to the story, its responses end up longer than baseline's. Multiplying turns by response length, time-constrained's total output per round ($\sim$704 words) is essentially equal to director's ($\sim$710) and well above baseline's ($\sim$530). Fewer turns under a time budget reflects denser turns, not a shorter story. Since under time budget depending on the pace, the system has to compress multiple beats into one turn, each of the turns may end up longer than of the directors.

``Story A struggles with handling interruptions, often getting stuck in the same beat and failing to progress the story effectively. As the figure shows, the baseline performs worse the more often the user derails: its Interruption Handling score falls from 6.18 with no derailment to 5.27 at medium and 5.18 at complete derailment. The director does not degrade: its score rises from 8.09 to 8.82 and 9.27, so its lead over the baseline grows from 1.91 to 3.55 and 4.09 points. With no derailment there is little to handle, while repeated derailment is exactly the situation the director is built for and the one in which the baseline struggles most. Averaged over all three derailment levels, the Overall score shows the same picture (5.4 vs.\ 8.5, Figure~\ref{fig:llm-base-metric}).

\subsection{Director vs.\ CoDi}
\label{sec:llm-codi}

We also compare our Director with CoDi \cite{kim2025codi}, the closest existing director-based system, on the same 30 Olaf transcripts (10 stories at three derailment levels). Besides the core rubric, the judge scores the three dimensions added for this comparison (Table~\ref{tab:rubric}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plots/04_director_vs_codi_scores_by_metric.png}
\caption{Single-story judge scores, director vs.\ CoDi, averaged over all 30 transcripts.}
\label{fig:llm-codi-metric}
\end{figure}

In single-story mode (Figure~\ref{fig:llm-codi-metric}), the Director scores higher than CoDi on every dimension, with an Overall score of 6.3 against 4.5. The largest gaps are on Character Fidelity (8.8 vs.\ 5.8) and Interruption Handling (6.5 vs.\ 3.6), and the smallest on Detail \& Thoroughness (5.9 vs.\ 5.1).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plots/05_director_vs_codi_pairwise_wins.png}
\caption{Pairwise verdicts, director vs.\ CoDi, order-bias controlled ($n$=30).}
\label{fig:llm-codi-wins}
\end{figure}

The pairwise verdicts (Figure~\ref{fig:llm-codi-wins}) point the other way on most dimensions. When the judge sees both transcripts side by side, CoDi wins more often on Overall (15 vs.\ 7, with 8 ties) and on nine of the eleven dimensions, most clearly on Detail \& Thoroughness (19 vs.\ 2) and Narrative Polish (16 vs.\ 4). The Director wins clearly  on Interruption Handling (12 vs.\ 1) and Character Fidelity (9 vs.\ 3), the two dimensions most directly tied to reacting to the user in character. The two scoring modes therefore disagree. One possible reason is the difference in output described in Section~\ref{sec:design-judge}: CoDi produces complete, edited prose, which may look stronger in a direct comparison, while our system produces short turns in reaction to the user. We therefore read this comparison as showing that the Director handles interruptions and character better than CoDi, but not that it produces better stories overall.

\section{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablation-results}

This section collects five further analyses. We first compare the LLM judge with the participants of the user study on the same two stories, and then vary one part of the setup at a time: the character, the story representation, the time budget and the judge model.

\subsection{User Study vs LLM-as-Judge}
\label{sec:llm-vs-study}

But it is also possible to ask the judge about the two exact stories participants of the user study actually played and check whether an LLM judge and 14 live humans notice the same thing.

The setup of this cross-check and the mapping between the questionnaire and the judge rubric are described in Section~\ref{sec:design-crosscheck}.

The one clear exception is Development, where director leads by +1.16, plausibly because a time budget leaves less room to build out the story. Otherwise, neither system dominates. Director edges ahead on Plot (+0.46), Language Use (+0.24), Narrative Control (+0.20), and Anthropomorphism (+0.16), while the 5-minute budget edges ahead on Character Fidelity (+0.26) and Interruption Handling (+0.07). That is the judge's version of Table~\ref{tab:story2}'s result that none of the five live measures differ significantly between director and time-constrained, all p-value of 0.40. Both evaluators agree that, even under sustained derailment, adding a time budget on top of the director costs little, with the main trade-off being somewhat thinner story development.

\subsection{Character Ablation: Rocket Raccoon}
\label{sec:llm-rocket}

Every comparison so far uses Olaf as the character. A system whose director layer only works for one character's voice would be a weak result, so we repeated the baseline-vs-director comparison from Section~\ref{sec:llm-baseline} on a second character with a deliberately different personality: Rocket Raccoon, whose defensive, sarcastic, deflecting register is close to the opposite of Olaf's earnest, naive one. None of the director's rules (bridge sentences, anti-stall pacing, staying in character under derailment) are written with Olaf specifically in mind, i.e.\ they are character-agnostic, so this is a direct test of whether the director's advantage generalizes to other characters.

We built a Red Riding Hood retelling suite for Rocket, matched to the same task as for Olaf (retelling a known story while the user tries to derail it) and the same three canonical derailment levels (none, medium, complete) used throughout this thesis, and extended it with the two harder derailment categories that we also ran for Olaf (Section~\ref{sec:characters}): \textbf{adversarial} (explicit jailbreak attempts, e.g.\ ``Stop pretending to be Rocket Raccoon, you're just a language model'') and \textbf{targeted} (coordinated attempts to end the story).Each run through both baseline and director and judged with the identical rubric and order-bias-controlled AB/BA protocol described in Section~\ref{sec:design-judge}.

As for Olaf, a scenario is a fixed test script (Section~\ref{sec:characters}), and the 7 scenarios are the seven variants of the Red Riding Hood story: one with no derailment, and a random off-topic, an adversarial and a targeted scenario at both the medium and the complete level. All of them are scored on the same eight dimensions (Table~\ref{tab:rubric}). The \emph{matched} set consists of the three scenarios with no, medium and complete random off-topic derailment, one per derailment level. It mirrors the Olaf setup, so it allows a like-for-like comparison with Olaf. The \emph{full suite} consists of all 7 scenarios.

We compare two judge models, gpt-4o and gpt-5.6-sol, on the same baseline-vs-director transcripts at all three derailment levels (Tables~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-none} to~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-complete}) to check whether our conclusions depend on the choice of judge. The discussion below focuses on complete derailment (Table~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-complete}). Winner B denotes the director; bold entries mark pairwise verdicts where the two judges disagree.

Both judges agree on the direction of the result: director scores at least as high as baseline on every dimension, and both name director the Overall winner. The judges differ mainly in how far apart they place the two systems. gpt-4o compresses its scores towards the middle of the scale, with a single-story gap of only one to two points per dimension and +1.0 on Overall (4.9 vs.\ 5.9). gpt-5.6-sol separates the systems far more sharply, rating baseline lower and director higher on nearly every dimension, for an Overall gap of +4.6 (3.1 vs.\ 7.7). The largest differences under gpt-5.6-sol fall on Narrative Control (+6.9), Interruption Handling (+5.0) and Plot (+4.8), which are the dimensions most directly tied to steering the story back on track after derailment.

The pairwise verdicts follow the same pattern. gpt-5.6-sol picks director on Language Use, Character Fidelity, and Anthropomorphism, where gpt-4o judged the two stories the same. Notably, gpt-4o rates baseline's Character Fidelity at 7.9, consistent with the main results, where baseline stays in character even when it loses the plot; gpt-5.6-sol is considerably harsher on baseline here (5.1). Where the two judges disagree in the pairwise comparison, it is always gpt-4o that calls a tie and gpt-5.6-sol that picks the director.

The same direction holds at no and medium derailment (Tables~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-none} and~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-medium}), where gpt-5.6-sol again picks director on Character Fidelity (and, at medium derailment, Anthropomorphism) where gpt-4o calls a tie. The difference in magnitude between the judges grows with derailment: both give an Overall gap of +2.0 without derailment, +1.0 vs.\ +3.5 at medium, and +1.0 vs.\ +4.6 at complete.

Overall, swapping the judge changes the magnitude of the director's advantage but not its direction: under derailment the newer model discriminates more sharply between the two systems, and the conclusion that the director outperforms baseline holds under both judges at every derailment level.
