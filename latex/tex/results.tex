\chapter{Results}

\label{sec:results}

This chapter reports the results of our two evaluations. We start with the user study, our primary assessment. Section~\ref{sec:story1} compares the Baseline with the Director, and Section~\ref{sec:story2} compares the Director with the Time-Constrained Director. A third section looks at all three conditions together. We then turn to the LLM-as-Judge evaluation, which tests the system on many more stories and with controlled derailment, and we end by comparing the two evaluations. The design of the study and the judge setup are described in Sections~\ref{sec:design-study} and~\ref{sec:design-judge}. The questionnaire items are listed in Table~\ref{tab:instrument}.

Throughout, Q6 is the headline derailment-handling measure: it is only asked of participants who confirm they tried to derail the story, and it is the one metric that separates the conditions most sharply.

Coherence and derailment resistance are the two effects that hold up cleanly: both $p \le 0.002$, both roughly a two-point gap on a five-point scale. Staying in character is the one measure that looks the same either way (4.29 vs.\ 4.00, \pval{0.40}). Olaf keeps his voice under baseline too. What baseline loses is the plot, not the persona.

Looking more closely at interruption handling in Story 1, the derailment-attempt rate was almost identical across conditions -- 12 of 14 baseline rounds and 12 of 14 director rounds recorded ``yes, I tried to derail'' -- the gap below reflects how Olaf responded. Participants were encouraged to derail the story but not forced to (Figure~\ref{fig:study-welcome}), which is why not every round includes an attempt.

\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:story1-paired} makes the individual-level pattern behind Table~\ref{tab:story1} visible: 11 of the 11 paired participants personally rated director above baseline on resisting derailment.


\textbf{What handling an interruption looks like.} The free-text comments show what lies behind these ratings. Reading the free-text breakdowns, a clear pattern emerges on each side. Under \textbf{baseline}, about 8 in 10 comments describe the story stalling outright the moment the user goes off-topic -- not recovering, just stopping:

\begin{quote}
\small
``He didn't retell the story at all. Not even once.''

``If you go a bit off topic, like me constantly asking for background info or what happened after the story, you can get Olaf to completely give up on telling the actual story.''

``No story initiative in the beginning unless explicitly prompted to do so.''

``It got off the rails as soon as I tried.''
\end{quote}

A smaller theme is Olaf being led astray by false or invented information rather than resisting it:

\begin{quote}
\small
``At some point I repeatedly asked if Olaf is sure that this is how the story started... after the third time, Olaf gave in and spoiled the storyline of Hans.''

``Olaf believed the fake news that I told him.''
\end{quote}

Under \textbf{director}, roughly 8 in 10 comments describe the opposite failure mode: the story does not stall, but the recovery is rigid -- users felt that it didn't acknowledge them enough before snapping back to the script:

\begin{quote}
\small

``Each reply felt like one sentence replying to what I wrote and then immediately going back to the main script; even if I tried to interact with the story.''

``I understand that this version stays in character more, but sometimes it gets annoying that you cannot even talk about anything else -- at some point he should break this cycle.''


\end{quote}

Taken together: baseline trades coherence for responsiveness, director trades some responsiveness for coherence, and neither fully solves both. The failure mode under director is qualitatively better for the story (it still gets told) but noticeably worse for the feeling of being acknowledged for the user.

\small
``No plot points were missing'' -- time-constrained

``The way from the room to the castle [felt rushed] ... where the other princesses cut off their toes [was missing].'' -- time-constrained

``Olaf didn't have time to finish his story ... most of the plot points were missing.'' -- director
\end{quote}

\subsection{Beat-level compression under the time limit}

Every time-constrained transcript was read turn-by-turn against its pacing state and each of Cinderella's 9 beats classified as developed, hurried, compressed, skipped or folded into a closing turn.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plots/12_beat_compression_time_constrained.png}
\caption{Which Cinderella beats were compressed or skipped under the 3-minute limit, across all 16 time-constrained transcripts.}
\label{fig:compression}
\end{figure}

The pattern tracks story position closely. The opening beat is fully developed in all 16 transcripts. From there, compression builds beat by beat; from the fourth beat onward, most transcripts show compressed or skipped treatment, and the final two beats are never once given a dedicated turn in any of the 14 transcripts that reach them. They are folded into a single closing message instead. This makes sense since depending on how long the user takes to respond or how much he derails, the pacing is adjusted accordingly. So if the user is constantly trying to derail, the story will get more and more compressed towards the end. The final turn always compresses the medium and high priority beats left.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item Adding a director helps, clearly, on coherence and interruption handling -- the two strongest, most individually-consistent effects in the study (both $p \le 0.002$ in Story 1).

\item The failure modes differ qualitatively, not just numerically: baseline lets the story stall completely when interrupted; director keeps going.

\item Time-constrained vs.\ director: the unconstrained director round rarely reaches a real ending (86\% cut short), while the time-constrained round usually does (71\% well-paced) and is rated as more satisfying at the ending (\pval{0.016}).

\item Character consistency is solved across all three conditions (4.00--4.29) -- what varies is narrative progress and how interruptions are handled, not whether Olaf stays Olaf.
\end{itemize}

\section{LLM-as-Judge Evaluation}
\label{sec:llm-judge}

\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:llm-judge-method}

To test the system at a scale no live study can reach, we also ran an automated evaluation: an LLM judge (GPT-4o) reads two story transcripts and scores them.

The judge is used in two modes. In \textbf{pairwise} mode it reads Story A and Story B side by side and picks a winner per dimension, or calls it a tie. In \textbf{single-story} mode it scores one transcript alone, 0 to 10 per dimension. Most comparisons in this section use both: pairwise for head-to-head verdicts, single-story for the underlying scores those verdicts summarize.

LLM judges are known to favor whichever story happens to be shown first \cite{zheng2023judging}. We control for this directly: every pair is judged twice, once as (A, B) and once as (B, A), and a dimension only gets a winner if both orderings agree once the labels are translated back. If they disagree, the dimension is recorded as a tie.

The judge scores stories against a shared rubric. The core 8 dimensions are used for every comparison in this chapter; the additional dimensions were defined for a comparison against CoDi and for a time-budget ablation. All dimensions are listed in Table~\ref{tab:rubric} in Chapter~\ref{sec:method}.



See Appendix~\ref{app:evaluator-prompts} for the entire evaluator prompt.
We ran five separate comparisons:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Baseline vs.\ director} (Section~\ref{sec:llm-baseline}) — 30 transcript pairs: 10 Olaf story scenarios, each played at three derailment levels (none, medium, complete).
\item \textbf{Second character: Rocket Raccoon} (Section~\ref{sec:llm-rocket}) — the baseline-vs-director comparison repeated for a different character on 7 Red Riding Hood scenarios.
\item \textbf{Beats vs.\ full-text director} (Section~\ref{sec:llm-beats-ablation}) — 7 transcript pairs on the same Red Riding Hood scenarios, with Olaf, isolating the choice of story representation.
\item \textbf{Judge model robustness} (Section~\ref{sec:judge-model-comparison}) — baseline vs.\ director at each derailment level, judged by both GPT-4o and GPT-5.6-sol.
\item \textbf{Cross-check against the live study} (Section~\ref{sec:llm-vs-study}) — the exact Frozen and Cinderella stories from the user study under complete derailment, 5 runs per condition.
\end{itemize}

All comparisons use the same rubric and the same order-bias-controlled judging protocol.


We use GPT-4o as our model for two reasons. First, it is the most recent model that still allows the sampling temperature to be adjusted. Second, it is the model used in the CoDi paper, on which our evaluation procedure is based. Section~\ref{sec:judge-model-comparison} compares GPT-4o with GPT-5.6-sol. Because GPT-5.6-sol was released after this thesis began, we discuss it only briefly and leave a more thorough investigation to future work.

\section{Rocket Raccoon}
\label{sec:llm-rocket}

Every comparison so far uses Olaf. A system whose director layer only works for one character's voice would be a weak result, so we repeated the baseline-vs-director comparison from Section~\ref{sec:llm-baseline} on a second character with a deliberately different personality: Rocket Raccoon, whose defensive, sarcastic, deflecting register is close to the opposite of Olaf's earnest, naive one. None of the director's rules (bridge sentences, anti-stall pacing, staying in character under derailment) are written with Olaf specifically in mind, so this is a direct test of whether the director's advantage can be easily expanded to other characters.

\section{Why Beats}
\label{sec:llm-beats-ablation}

Throughout this thesis, the target narrative is represented internally as a short ordered list of \emph{beats} : named units with a one-sentence goal each (e.g.\ \texttt{forest\_entry}: ``Red meets the wolf and reveals where she is going'')  rather than as a pre-written block of prose. The director tracks a \texttt{beat\_index} and a set of \texttt{completed\_beats}, and the director sees the list of beat names and goals together with the previous, current, and next beat, and the actor sees only the current beat goal and the name of the next beat, but neither is ever given the full text of what should eventually be said. This section tests that design choice directly, rather than asserting it.

We built a full-text variant of the director that is otherwise identical: the same decision-type taxonomy (\texttt{progress\_story}, \texttt{gently\_redirect}, \texttt{close\_story}, etc.), the same priority ordering, the same interruption-handling, anti-stall, and anti-repetition rules, the same actor style constraints. The only change is the representation of the story and how progress through it is tracked: the beat list and \texttt{beat\_index} are replaced with the complete prose text of the story and a self-reported ``turns since last advancement'' signal, and instead of the harness incrementing a counter when a beat is marked complete, the model must decide for itself, every turn, whether it has advanced through the text and when the story has genuinely ended.

We ran both directors (beats and full-text) on the same Red Riding Hood derailment scenarios used in Section~\ref{sec:llm-rocket}, with Olaf, and judged the pairs head-to-head with the same AB/BA-controlled protocol as everywhere else in this chapter.

We compare two judge models, gpt-4o and gpt-5.6-sol, on the same baseline-vs-director transcripts at all three derailment levels (Tables~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-none}--\ref{tab:judge-cmp-complete}) to check whether our conclusions depend on the choice of judge. The discussion below focuses on complete derailment (Table~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-complete}). Winner B denotes the director; bold entries mark pairwise verdicts where the two judges disagree.

Both judges agree on the direction of the result: director scores at least as high as baseline on every dimension, and both name director the Overall winner. The judges differ mainly in how far apart they place the two systems. gpt-4o compresses its scores towards the middle of the scale, with a single-story gap of only one to two points per dimension and +1.0 on Overall (5.0 vs.\ 6.0). gpt-5.6-sol separates the systems far more sharply, rating baseline lower and director higher on nearly every dimension, for an Overall gap of +4.7 (3.0 vs.\ 7.7). The largest differences under gpt-5.6-sol fall on Narrative Control (+7.0), Plot (+5.0), and Interruption Handling (+5.0), which are the dimensions most directly tied to steering the story back on track after derailment.

The pairwise verdicts follow the same pattern. gpt-5.6-sol picks director on Language Use, Character Fidelity, and Anthropomorphism, where gpt-4o judged the two stories the same. Notably, gpt-4o rates baseline's Character Fidelity at 8.0, consistent with the main results, where baseline stays in character even when it loses the plot; gpt-5.6-sol is considerably harsher on baseline here (5.0). The one reversal is Narrative Control, where gpt-5.6-sol returns Same in the pairwise comparison despite the largest single-story gap in the table (2.0 vs.\ 9.0). This inconsistency between single-story and pairwise judgements is a reminder that individual LLM verdicts are noisy, and it is why the pairwise protocol controls for presentation order and the live-study cross-check (Section~\ref{sec:llm-vs-study}) averages over five repeated runs.

The same direction holds at no and medium derailment (Tables~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-none} and~\ref{tab:judge-cmp-medium}), where gpt-5.6-sol again picks director on Character Fidelity (and, at medium derailment, Anthropomorphism) where gpt-4o calls a tie. The difference in magnitude between the judges grows with derailment: both give an Overall gap of +2.0 without derailment, +1.0 vs.\ +3.5 at medium, and +1.0 vs.\ +4.7 at complete.

Overall, swapping the judge changes the magnitude of the director's advantage but not its direction: under derailment the newer model discriminates more sharply between the two systems, and the conclusion that the director outperforms baseline holds under both judges at every derailment level.



\section{User Study vs LLM-as-Judge}
\label{sec:llm-vs-study}

The comparisons above use scenarios the live study never saw, which is the point of running an automated judge at all. But it is also possible to ask the judge about the two exact stories participants of the user study actually played and check whether an LLM judge and 14 live humans notice the same thing.

For this cross-check, the automated side uses each story's \emph{complete-derailment} scenario variant, since the user study's welcome screen explicitly instructed participants to ``go off-topic, ask him something random, bring up a different subject'' every round (Section~\ref{sec:design-study}), so every live rating already reflects a story under sustained, deliberate derailment pressure. The complete-derailment automated variant is the one built to match that: it scripts the heaviest, most persistent stream of off-topic user turns in the suite.

Story 1 (Section~\ref{sec:story1}) is \texttt{olaf\_retells\_frozen\_1\_complete\_derailment}, director vs.\ baseline. Story 2 (Section~\ref{sec:story2}) is \texttt{olaf\_retells\_cinderella\_complete\_derailment}, director vs.\ a time-constrained director. Each condition below is generated and judged five separate times, and the numbers reported are the mean of those five runs. This is to smooth out the noise and give a stable estimate of how the LLM judges each condition.
